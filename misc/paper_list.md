# Paper Critique: the reading list

EMSC3025 / EMSC6025 — Water in the Earth System

There are twenty-two papers below and twenty-two of you, so everyone ends up
with one. You choose your own, first come first served, by signing yourself into
the matching group under People and then Paper Critique Sign-up on Canvas. One
student per paper. Canvas will not stop a second person joining a group that is
already taken, so check before you join; if two of you end up on the same paper,
the later sign-up will be asked to move. Nothing is allocated for you, so it
pays to look through this list early rather than the night before. If you have a
strong reason to critique something that is not here, come and talk to me before
you start reading.

Every paper is linked from the Paper Critique Presentation assignment page on
Canvas, under the filename given with each entry below. The assessment is a talk
and nothing else: ten
minutes to present plus about five for questions, marked out of 20. There is no
written report and no peer review this year. Most of your ten minutes should be
your own analysis rather than a summary of what the authors did, so read the
assessment guidelines alongside this list. Postgraduate students taking EMSC6025
also need to find two later papers that cite the one they picked, and say
something about why those authors cited it.

A note on how these were chosen. Every paper here is Earth science: somebody
went out and measured something about the planet, or built a physical model of
it, and then argued about what the measurement means. That is deliberate. It is
much easier to write a sharp critique of a paper that makes a specific,
falsifiable claim than of a broad review, so I have kept reviews to a minimum.
The list also spreads across the whole water cycle rather than sitting entirely
on groundwater depletion, which means the topics range from crustal deformation
to river water isotopes to satellite mapping of floods.

## Groundwater and the solid Earth

These papers treat water as a mass load. Take water out of an aquifer and the
crust underneath rises, the geoid changes shape, the sea surface tilts, and in
one case the stress on a major fault changes. If you are drawn to geophysics
rather than hydrology, start here.

**1. Amos et al. (2014)**, _Nature_ — `amos2014.pdf`, doi `10.1038/nature13275`

Continuous GPS across central California records 1 to 3 mm per year of uplift
around the southern San Joaquin Valley. The authors argue that this is flexure
of the lithosphere responding to a century and a half of groundwater removal,
and that the resulting seasonal unloading modulates stress on the San Andreas
Fault and therefore microseismicity. The causal chain runs from irrigation
pumping all the way to earthquakes, which is a long chain with several places
to push on it. Ask yourself how well the simple elastic model is justified, and
whether the seismicity correlation is as strong as the framing suggests.

**2. Borsa, Agnew & Cayan (2014)**, _Science_ — `borsa2014.pdf`, doi `10.1126/science.1260279`

The western United States dried out badly from 2013, and the solid Earth
responded by rising. Using seasonally adjusted GPS time series the authors
measure a median uplift of 5 mm, up to 15 mm in the mountains of California,
and invert that displacement field for the missing water mass, arriving at a
deficit of roughly 240 gigatonnes. Four pages long, one clean idea, one
inversion. Because it is so compact, everything the argument rests on is
visible, which makes it unusually good material for a critique.

**3. Veit & Conrad (2016)**, _Geophysical Research Letters_ — `veit2016.pdf`, doi `10.1002/2016GL068118`

Groundwater depletion has raised global mean sea level by up to about 25 mm,
but it does not raise it evenly. Unloading the continents lifts the solid
surface and pulls the geoid down nearby, so sea level rise is actually slower
along coasts next to heavily depleted aquifers. The paper models this and finds
deviations of about −0.4 mm per year off western North America and southern
Asia, then compares against tide gauges. A good example of a paper whose result
is counter-intuitive until you think about gravity properly.

**4. Konikow (2011)**, _Geophysical Research Letters_ — `konikow2011.pdf`, doi `10.1029/2011GL048604`

A bookkeeping paper: how much groundwater has humanity removed since 1900, and
what did that do to sea level. The answer is about 4500 km³, equivalent to
12.6 mm of sea level rise, with the rate accelerating sharply after 1950. The
estimate is assembled from calibrated groundwater models, analytical
approaches, and volumetric budgets for many separate aquifer systems, which
means the uncertainty is very hard to pin down. Working out how much you
believe the total, and why, is most of the critique. Pairs naturally with
papers 3 and 8 if you want to argue about the sea level budget.

**5. Chaussard et al. (2014)**, _Remote Sensing of Environment_ — `chaussard2014.pdf`, doi `10.1016/j.rse.2013.08.038`

InSAR time series from ALOS covering all of central Mexico rather than just
Mexico City, finding subsidence in 21 separate areas, exceeding 30 cm per year
in the capital. The interesting part is not the rates but the structure: the
sharpest velocity gradients sit on pre-existing faults, so groundwater
extraction is driving motion on faults that tectonics is not currently moving.
Consider what the technique can and cannot see, and how confidently land use
can be linked to subsidence category.

**6. Herrera-García et al. (2021)**, _Science_ — `herrera-garcia2021.pdf`, doi `10.1126/science.abb8549`

A global susceptibility map for subsidence caused by groundwater depletion,
built from a systematic literature review combined with a statistical model,
concluding that nineteen percent of the world's population lives where
subsidence is likely. Note that this is a Policy Forum piece rather than a
standard research article, which shapes what the authors were able to include.
The training data are the obvious place to aim a critique: what does a global
model learn when the underlying case studies are concentrated in a handful of
well-studied countries?

## Satellite gravity and total water storage

The GRACE mission measures where mass is on the Earth, month by month. These
papers use it to weigh continents, and they disagree with each other in
interesting ways.

**7. Famiglietti et al. (2011)**, _Geophysical Research Letters_ — `famiglietti2011.pdf`, doi `10.1029/2010GL046442`

Six and a half years of GRACE data over the Sacramento and San Joaquin basins,
showing water loss of 31 mm per year, of which the authors attribute 20 mm to
groundwater depletion in the Central Valley. The attribution step is the one
worth examining, since separating groundwater from soil moisture and snow
requires a hydrological model that GRACE itself cannot check. An early and much
cited demonstration of what satellite gravity can do for water management.

**8. Reager et al. (2016)**, _Science_ — `reager2016.pdf`, doi `10.1126/science.aad8386`

Between 2002 and 2014 the continents gained about 3200 gigatonnes of water
because of climate variability, and that gain quietly slowed sea level rise by
0.71 mm per year. In other words, land hydrology has been hiding part of the
sea level signal, and it had been left out of IPCC budgets because nobody could
measure it. The result depends on cleanly separating glacier loss from land
water gain within the same gravity field, so look closely at how that
disaggregation was done.

**9. Long et al. (2013)**, _Geophysical Research Letters_ — `long2013.pdf`, doi `10.1002/grl.50655`

The 2011 Texas drought seen by GRACE, one of the largest single year water
storage losses ever recorded from space. A short, focused case study, which
makes it a good choice if you would rather critique one well-defined event than
a global synthesis. Think about the spatial resolution of GRACE relative to the
area being discussed, and what that does to the numbers.

**10. Rodell et al. (2018)**, _Nature_ — `rodell2018.pdf`, doi `10.1038/s41586-018-0123-1`

Thirteen years of GRACE assembled into a global picture of where freshwater is
being gained and lost, with each of 34 trend regions attributed to a cause:
climate variability, climate change, or direct human intervention. The
ambition is what makes it critiqueable, because attribution over such a short
record is genuinely difficult and the authors are candid about it. Watch how
the paper handles the difference between a trend and a slow oscillation.

**11. Leblanc et al. (2009)**, _Water Resources Research_ — `leblanc2009.pdf`, doi `10.1029/2008WR007333`

The Murray-Darling Basin during the Millennium Drought, tracked through the
whole hydrological cycle at once by combining GRACE with in situ groundwater
levels and modelled soil moisture. Surface water and soil moisture bottomed out
within two years, while groundwater kept falling for at least six, a loss of
104 km³, and was still falling after rainfall returned to average. This is the
basin your assignments use, so you will already know the setting.

## Aquifers, water age, and the subsurface

Papers about the groundwater itself: how deep it is, how old it is, how fast it
moves, and how quickly it responds to climate.

**12. Fan, Li & Miguez-Macho (2013)**, _Science_ — `fan2013.pdf`, doi `10.1126/science.1229881`

The first global map of water table depth, built from about 1.6 million well
observations plus a groundwater model to fill the very large gaps. Shallow
groundwater turns out to influence 22 to 32 percent of global land area, which
reframes groundwater as something ecosystems depend on daily rather than a deep
reserve. The observations are spread extremely unevenly across the world, so
the honest question is how much of the published map is data and how much is
model. Note that this is a Science offprint, so the first page carries the end
of an unrelated article before Fan's paper begins.

**13. Gleeson et al. (2016)**, _Nature Geoscience_ — `gleeson2016.pdf`, doi `10.1038/ngeo2590`

How much groundwater is there, and how much of it is young enough to matter on
human timescales? Using tritium from atmospheric weapons testing as a clock,
the authors find about 22.6 million km³ of groundwater in the upper 2 km of
crust, but less than 6 percent of it recharged within the last fifty years. The
tritium method and the way it is scaled up globally are both worth
interrogating.

**14. Jasechko et al. (2017)**, _Nature Geoscience_ — `jasechko2017.pdf`, doi `10.1038/ngeo2943`

Most groundwater pumped from deep wells is fossil water, infiltrated before the
Holocene, and everybody assumed fossil water was safely isolated from modern
pollution. Using carbon-14 and tritium together, this paper shows that a
surprising fraction of fossil-dominated wells also contain detectable modern
water, meaning old does not mean protected. A neat example of a result that
overturns an assumption rather than adding a number. The file is the accepted
manuscript rather than the typeset version, so the figures sit at the end.

**15. Jasechko et al. (2024)**, _Nature_ — `jasechko2024.pdf`, doi `10.1038/s41586-023-06879-8`

Groundwater levels measured directly in about 170,000 monitoring wells across
1693 aquifer systems, rather than inferred from satellites. Declines faster
than 0.5 m per year are widespread, and in 30 percent of regional aquifers the
decline has accelerated over the past four decades. What lifts it above the
usual bad news is the second half: the authors identify aquifers where the
trend reversed after a policy change or a managed recharge scheme, so recovery
is demonstrably possible. Ask what the well network can and cannot represent.

**16. Cuthbert et al. (2019)**, _Nature Climate Change_ — `cuthbert2019.pdf`, doi `10.1038/s41558-018-0386-4`

Aquifers do not respond to a change in rainfall immediately; they have a
memory, and the length of that memory varies enormously. Combining global
groundwater model results with hydrological datasets, the authors show that
roughly half of global groundwater flux could re-equilibrate with changed
recharge within about a century, while arid regions respond far more slowly
than humid ones. The consequence is uncomfortable: today's climate impacts on
rivers and groundwater-dependent ecosystems may be hidden for decades.

**17. Ketabchi et al. (2016)**, _Journal of Hydrology_ — `ketabchi2016.pdf`, doi `10.1016/j.jhydrol.2016.01.083`

The one substantial review on the list, covering how sea level rise pushes
saltwater into coastal aquifers. It is here because the physics of the
freshwater-saltwater interface is central to the course and because the
literature genuinely disagrees, with some studies finding sea level rise
dominant and others finding pumping dominant. Reviewing a review is harder than
critiquing a research paper, so take this one only if you are comfortable
arguing about how the authors weighed conflicting evidence.

## Rivers, evaporation, and water at the surface

**18. Jasechko et al. (2016)**, _Nature Geoscience_ — `jasechko2016.pdf`, doi `10.1038/ngeo2636`

Seasonal cycles of oxygen-18 in rain, snow and streamflow from 254 catchments
worldwide, used to work out what fraction of river water fell as precipitation
within the past two or three months. About a third of global river discharge
turns out to be that young, even though mean transit times in the same
catchments are years to decades. Since young streamflow comes from less than
0.1 percent of global groundwater storage, a very thin layer of aquifer
controls a great deal of stream chemistry. The isotope method here is exactly
what we cover in the runoff tutorials.

**19. Jung et al. (2010)**, _Nature_ — `jung2010.pdf`, doi `10.1038/nature09396`

Global land evapotranspiration rose steadily from 1982 to 1997 and then stopped
rising, and the authors argue the cause is soil moisture running short in the
Southern Hemisphere, with Australia and Africa doing most of the work. The
estimate combines flux tower measurements, satellite data and a machine
learning algorithm, then checks the result against process-based land surface
models. Whether an eleven-year flattening in a 27-year record is a real change
or natural variability is the obvious line of attack, and the authors say so
themselves. The file has an eScholarship cover page before the article.

**20. Mueller et al. (2016)**, _Remote Sensing of Environment_ — `mueller2016.pdf`, doi `10.1016/j.rse.2015.11.003`

Twenty-five years of Landsat imagery processed into a map of where surface
water has ever been observed across the whole of Australia, which is the
foundation of what is now a national operational product. The critique here is
methodological rather than conceptual: how do you separate water from cloud
shadow, terrain shadow and wet soil at continental scale, and how do you
validate a map for which no independent truth exists?

**21. Glenn et al. (2011)**, _Hydrological Processes_ — `glenn2011.pdf`, doi `10.1002/hyp.8391`

How actual evapotranspiration is measured in Australia, covering eddy
covariance towers, sap flow sensors, catchment water balance and the various
remote sensing approaches, with the honest conclusion that the best models
still carry 10 to 20 percent uncertainty. Roughly 90 percent of Australian
rainfall returns to the atmosphere, so this is the largest and least
well-constrained term in the national water balance. A survey paper, so the
same warning applies as for paper 17.

**22. van Dijk et al. (2013)**, _Water Resources Research_ — `vandijk2013.pdf`, doi `10.1002/wrcr.20123`

The definitive account of the Millennium Drought in southeast Australia,
covering causes and consequences across climate, hydrology, ecosystems and the
economy. El Niño explains about two thirds of the rainfall deficit in the east;
for the south the authors say plainly that a climate change contribution
remains plausible but unproven, which is a refreshing thing to find in a paper.
River management protected some water users by shifting the damage onto
irrigators and onto river ecosystems. Long and wide-ranging, so if you take it,
pick two or three threads and go deep rather than trying to cover everything.

## Choosing well

Papers 2, 3, 4, 9 and 18 are short and tightly argued, which makes them the
easiest to fit into ten minutes without rushing. Papers 6, 20, 21 and 22 are
broad, and will need you to select ruthlessly. If you want to be able to say
something about how the field responded, the older papers give you more
citations to work with; if you would rather critique something the community
has not settled yet, papers 6, 15 and 16 are the most recent.

Several papers argue with each other, and using that is a good way to make a
critique concrete rather than vague. Papers 3, 4 and 8 are three different
accounts of how land water affects sea level. Papers 7, 11 and 15 measure
groundwater depletion by three different methods, satellite gravity, combined
observation, and monitoring wells, and do not entirely agree. Papers 11 and 22
describe the same Australian drought from two directions.
